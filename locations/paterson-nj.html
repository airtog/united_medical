What Happens at Your Exam

The appointment itself is more routine than most people expect. We start by checking your photo identification and going through whatever immunization records you've brought. Dr. Weiss then performs the physical examination USCIS requires — a general assessment covering your eyes, ears, heart, lungs, abdomen, skin and neurological function. Blood is drawn and a urine sample collected in our own office for the required laboratory testing, and tuberculosis screening is completed at the same visit. Finally we go through which vaccinations USCIS requires at your age against what your records already show. Most Paterson patients are in and out in about half an hour.

Documents to Bring to Your Appointment

Three things matter most: valid government photo identification (an unexpired passport is best, a driver's license works), any immunization records you hold — foreign-language records are perfectly fine — and documentation of any significant medical history, particularly a previous positive TB test or treatment for a chronic condition. Add a written list of medications you currently take. If you have already printed Form I-693, leave it unsigned; the signature must be made in front of the civil surgeon. Relatives and interpreters are welcome to accompany you.

Timing Your Exam Correctly

Getting the timing right saves money. USCIS stopped applying a calendar expiry to forms signed on or after November 1, 2023, but policy effective June 11, 2025 ties each completed I-693 to the single application it was filed with — it will not carry over to a future petition, and a denial or withdrawal means a new exam. Anything signed before November 1, 2023 remains under the old two-year rule. So the sensible pattern for Paterson applicants is to book the exam once the rest of the application package is ready, or when USCIS asks for it, rather than months ahead.

Why Accuracy Is What You're Actually Paying For

Paterson has one of the largest immigrant populations in New Jersey, and the filings that come out of it are rarely simple. An I-693 is a legal document that a physician happens to complete, and its only job is to be accepted by USCIS the first time. In our experience the exams that come back rejected fail for administrative reasons far more often than medical ones — a physician who was not USCIS-designated, an edition of the form USCIS no longer accepts, a missing or mismatched signature, dates that disagree, an incomplete tuberculosis classification, a blank vaccination section, or an envelope whose seal was broken before USCIS opened it. Any one of those can produce a Request for Evidence, and an RFE means returning to the civil surgeon while your case sits. Since June 2025 the consequences are heavier still: a completed I-693 is valid only for the application it was filed with, so a denied or withdrawn case means paying for an entirely new exam. That is the real risk Paterson applicants are managing, and it is what our $599 is for.

It is also what immigration attorneys care about most. An attorney has usually spent months assembling a filing, and the medical exam is one of the few pieces they hand to someone outside their office — so what they care about is that the sealed form comes back complete, consistent and on the current edition, without generating an RFE that delays everything else.
